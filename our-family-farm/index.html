Our family’s story on Tubbs Road began with a small dairy farm. The Christmas trees came later, when Ronald Granger saw that the farm needed a new direction.

Our family’s story

In 1969, Ronald and Esther Granger bought a small dairy farm from relatives in Mexico, New York. They planned to keep milking cows, but as more small dairies closed, Ronald could see the choice ahead: grow the dairy to keep it going, or find a different way to farm the land.

Ronald chose to grow Christmas trees. He and his son Randy planted their first trees in the early 1980s and spent years preparing and tending the fields. That work paid off in the winter of 1997, when the family sold its first Christmas tree. Ronald taught his sons how to care for the land and keep the tree fields in shape, passing on what he’d learned from years of working the farm.

Randy and Ray grew up milking cows alongside their father. Ray went on to learn how to build houses and other structures, while Randy got to know the land on Tubbs Road and what it could grow. That first season, the family worked out of a small shed across the road from the old farmhouse, right beside the heifer barn. They started small and built on what they had each year. The old heifer barn is now home to the farm kitchen, and that original shed still stands behind the barn. During Christmas season, it holds the sleds, saws, and wreaths. Ray built and maintained the buildings the farm needed as it grew, and Randy used what he’d learned about the land to grow Christmas trees and, eventually, pumpkins. You could see the years of hard work Ronald, Randy, and Ray put in, from the rows of Christmas trees to the buildings that went up as the farm grew.

Esther ran the farm kitchen for years. She took pride in keeping it clean and tidy, and her sweet and sassy personality made stopping in part of the tradition for many families.

Then there was her fudge. To the day she died, Esther would tell you hers was the best. People still stop in and ask for it today.

Ronald and Esther have both passed away, and their family carries on the work they started. Granger’s Farm is still a small family farm, where we work together to grow Christmas trees and pumpkins, care for our Nigerian dwarf goats, and welcome visitors each season.

During Christmas season, you’ll often find Randy chatting with customers or hustling around the farm in bright colors and festive Christmas gear. Ray keeps the day-to-day work moving, looking after the buildings and equipment and making sure everything works the way it should.

The original Hallmark movie started right here on Tubbs Road. Andrea, Randy’s oldest daughter, grew up working on the farm. It made her grandfather proud to see the care Andrea put into her work. He liked to brag about how well she shaped the Christmas trees and how clean a field looked after she’d been through with the bush hog. She spent a few years in the city learning how businesses run, then brought what she learned back home. Today, she runs the farm kitchen as her grandmother did and makes decisions about the goat herd. She’s still learning from her dad about the land and growing Christmas trees, and from her uncle about the equipment, buildings, and work that keep the farm running. If you can’t find Andrea in the kitchen, she’s probably in the goat pen sorting out whatever the goats got into this time.

Charlize, Randy’s youngest daughter, is part of the farm’s third generation. With so many years between her and the rest of her generation, it feels a little like watching a fourth generation grow up on the farm. Right now, she loves spending time on the farm and learning from her family, a little at a time. We hope she’ll want to be part of it someday, and she has plenty of time to figure out what she wants to do.

Ronald and Esther’s family spent years planting and tending trees before they sold their first one. Starting a Christmas tree farm today means paying for land, seedlings, equipment, and labor for years before there’s a crop to sell. Sometimes it’s the next generation that gets to see what all that work made possible.

Randy is still one of the youngest in the room at Christmas tree growers’ meetings. It’s a reminder of how much depends on having someone willing and able to take over. When a farm closes, it’s hard to start another in its place. That’s why it means so much to see the younger members of our family taking an interest. We hope the work they learn here will give another generation of families the chance to walk these same rows and find their own Christmas tree.

We’re glad to share this place with your family, whether you’re visiting for the first time or coming back for a tradition of your own.

How we grow your Christmas tree

Long before you walk the rows looking for your tree, it starts as a little seedling in one of our fields. A Christmas tree can take seven to ten years to reach a size that’s ready to take home. In the same field, you’ll see little trees just getting started and taller ones that are nearly ready for Christmas.

A little care, year after year

We prepare the ground before we plant and keep the grass and weeds down while the young trees put down roots. Every growing season, we mow between the rows. Once the trees are old enough, we trim their new growth each year to encourage fuller branches and help shape them. As mature trees are cut, there’s room to plant another generation. More on growing Christmas trees from Cornell Cooperative Extension.

A real tree after Christmas

While they grow, Christmas trees absorb carbon dioxide and can provide shelter for wildlife. A real tree is made of wood and needles that break down naturally. Once the decorations and tinsel are off, a tree recycling program can turn it into mulch. When you buy a tree from a local farm, you help support the people who plant and tend those fields year after year. More on real Christmas trees from Penn State Extension.
